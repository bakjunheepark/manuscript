THE MODERNIST GRID

Tell me, are you a Gridnik?

Richard Hollis, 2004 THE MODERNIST GRID

Aesthetic or structure

"The concept of proportion implies aesthetics. In the visual culture of Modernism, aesthetics and function were, ideally, reconciled. The work of stereotype ﬁgures of this culture — of the painter Mondrian and the architect Mies - suggests something about the grid. Horizontal and vertical lines divide the painter’s canvases and the architect’s façades — deﬁning Mondrian’s panels of ﬂat colour and Mies’ rectangles of glass or cladding. It is an image of rectangularity.

Yet, in relation to the grid in print design, the examples of Mondrian and Mies are deceptive, but at the same time instructive. It is only its rectangularity that connects Mondrian’s work with the grid: its relationships, the width of its lines and the areas of its planes are arrived at intuitively, by experiment. It provided a formal language: its function was purely aesthetic."

Richard Hollis

As someone with a background in architecture who is now majoring in Communication Design, this was a very interesting read, since I had never considered the “grid” as an element of design before. What struck me most was the comparison between Mondrian and Mies: one using rectangles as an aesthetic choice, the other using them out of structural necessity for the building. This is the part that made me realize why I had never even considered the grid to be “design” in the first place.

Crossing disciplines

"Mies was the third director of the Bauhaus. His two predecessors, Walter Gropius and Hannes Meyer, were architects with an interest in industrialised building. Yet the school showed no equivalent concern with the inherent mathematics of typography. And Tschichold, the greatest spokesman and exponent of Modernist typography, in spite of his concern with proportions and type area, seems unaware of horizontal divisions of the type area and indifferent to the problems of organising text and image — apart from posters.

The Bauhaus founder Gropius later demonstrated the principles of the standardisation necessary for prefabricated construction by illustrating the Japanese system of tatami mats — a grid-like system of modular units with a separating space."

Richard Hollis

I loved seeing how the two disciplines cross over, from Max Bill moving between architecture and graphic design, to Gropius, the Bauhaus founder, referencing Japanese tatami mats as a precedent for modular systems. Or the Swiss designers responding to their own specific conditions, shaped by an architecture heavy design culture.

Gradual or a rule?

"The grid was a concept that emerged only gradually. It seems almost eccentric that Bill, the chief pioneer of the typographical grid, did not realise its value earlier. Bill, as well as being an experienced graphic designer, was an architect and an admirer of Le Corbusier, who had been working on his Modulor from 1942 until its publication in 1948. Bill designed several volumes recording Le Corbusier’s work. With the ﬁrst, published in 1939 in a horizontal three-column format, he made no attempt to standardise the sizes of illustration. In the next volume, published in 1946, while there is some consistency in the height of horizontal photographs at the bottom of the page, there is no systematic control. Yet in The New Architecture, a book Bill had designed in 1940, the photographs are restricted to the depths and widths prescribed by the grid of 3x3 rectangles. When Bill came to design his own book on the bridge builder Robert Maillart, he reused some of the photoengraved blocks from The New Architecture. With a squarer format and a grid of two columns plus a half column, Bill provided himself with an astonishingly ﬂexible system that allowed him to organise photographs in several sizes and proportions with technical drawings, text and captions."

Richard Hollis

However, I’m not sure I agree with Hollis’s claim that the grid was a concept that only emerged gradually. He frames it as a mostly accidental byproduct of practical constraints. Maybe that’s true early on, but once it crystallized through the Swiss Werkbund, it quickly became a rule for designers to follow, rather than a structure that emerges naturally from the content.

Invisible today

"Printing until the middle of the twentieth century was mainly letterpress, from inked type and photoengravings. The piece of metal which carried the inked letter was rectangular. These rectangles increased in height with the size of the letter in ﬁxed increments, so that moveable, reusable type has been described as a modular, standardised system."

Richard Hollis

The grid feels invisible and unnoticeable today, since nobody uses rectangular metal type to print or design anymore. But if I think about it, font size, spacing systems, and column length all relate to the same underlying constraints the grid once imposed. The difference is that the grid Hollis describes was built to solve a fixed, static problem: organizing type and image on a printed page. I don’t think that can be applied the same way in contemporary interaction design, since our designs are constantly evolving with user engagement and response. The grid may need to resize and reorder with every movement the user makes.

Going forward

"The essential elements of the grid — rectangularity, standardisation and modules, repetition and separation — are all present in pre-Modernist typographic design."

Richard Hollis,2004

This reading got me thinking about the concept of the grid in a way I never had before, and going forward, I’ll be more conscious of the choices I make around what constraints, standardization, repetition, I bring into a design, and why.
